A note on my process

Why I Built Water Protectors

Dear reader,

I’m a third grade teacher in Florida, and I have been studying the Ontario mathematics curriculum on my own. Nobody assigned it. I wanted to understand it from the inside before I ever write for it.

I started with Strand A, Social-Emotional Learning Skills in Mathematics. Florida’s standards don’t have anything like it, and it pulled me in right away. I struggled with math as a student, and in ten years of teaching I have watched children decide, before a lesson even starts, that math isn’t for them. Mindset is the gate. A child who believes they can’t access math shuts down and doesn’t get what they need. That is the gap I care about.

Then, in the Grade 3 curriculum, I found an example linking multiplication to water bottles. A student explains that their community can drink from the tap, so they use refillable bottles, and then adds:

“But not all communities in Canada have clean drinking water.”

I had just written about Flint for my social policy coursework at Trinity College Dublin, so seeing clean water show up in a Grade 3 math lesson made me feel completely aligned. I loved that Ontario put it there.

But when I read it again, I noticed who was speaking. The voice belongs to a child whose community has clean water. The child whose community doesn’t is only “not all communities”: talked about, never speaking. And the plastic bottles are framed as a habit to give up, when for some families bottled water is the only safe option. A child living under a drinking water advisory could hear that lesson and come away feeling that their family is the problem.

So I played with it and turned the whole thing on its head:

  • Inquiry first, not deficit. The lesson opens with a question: where does fresh water come from? Every child can contribute, and nobody starts out as “the one without.” Then the class learns that Canada holds about 20% of the world’s fresh water.
  • No guilt about bottled water. I would never place that on a child.
  • Kids are the problem solvers. How much water does a household need? A community? How do we make it safe to drink? The multiplication and division have a real purpose.
  • Responsibility goes where it belongs. If a third grader can work out what a community needs, the people making decisions can make sure every community has it. The children’s math becomes their evidence.
  • Water protectors, not victims. Students meet Josephine Mandamin and Autumn Peltier, Anishinaabe water protectors from Wiikwemkoong, Ontario. Protecting water is shown as leadership and strength, which the curriculum itself suggests.

To show that the idea holds up across a real classroom, I built it out as a full kit: whole group slides and a packet, the small-group phase packets I use for intervention (Pre-Phase through Phase 3), an enrichment project, and a Logic Lab sheet where a repeat loop becomes multiplication. It is aligned to Grade 3 expectations B2.1, B2.2, B2.6 and B2.7.

There is still something I need to learn. I don’t yet know enough about how First Nations schools use the Ontario curriculum, and I believe lessons like this should be designed with communities, not about them. That is where I want to go next.

This lesson is an example of the work I want to do: curriculum where children in underserved communities see themselves in the math as capable and proud, and are never a blind spot.

A note on how I work: the problem solving and the design are mine. I use AI tools to help produce the materials, such as drafting practice problems and laying out pages, so I can spend my time on the design thinking.

Thank you for reading.

Genevieve Campuzano Garrido
Genna · Grade 3 educator
