A note on my process

Why I Built Water Protectors

Dear reader,

I’m a third grade teacher in Florida, and I’m working toward moving to Toronto and building curriculum in Ontario. Part of getting there is learning the system, so I’ve been studying the Ontario mathematics curriculum strand by strand.

I started with Strand A, Social-Emotional Learning Skills in Mathematics, and I was really drawn to it. I struggled with math as a student, and I believe mindset matters: a child who goes into math believing they can’t access it is more likely to shut down and miss what they need.

In the Grade 3 curriculum, I came across an example connecting multiplication to water bottles. A student talks about how their community can drink from the tap, so they use refillable bottles, and then adds:

“But not all communities in Canada have clean drinking water.”

I loved seeing this in a math lesson. I had recently written about Flint for my social policy coursework at Trinity College Dublin, so it felt really aligned with what I care about. The example asks children to think about life outside their own bubble, and some children will also see their own lives in it.

That’s where I started thinking. For a child whose community is under a drinking water advisory, hearing it this way could be painful. So my question became: how do we make sure that child feels a sense of pride in this lesson, too?

I decided to play around with it and flip it on its head:

  • Start with inquiry. The lesson opens with a question every child can answer: where does fresh water come from? From there, the class explores the geography of Canada, which holds about 20% of the world’s fresh water.
  • Leave out any guilt. Bottled water is never framed as a bad habit. For some families, it’s what keeps them safe.
  • Let kids be the problem solvers. How much water does a household need? A whole community? How do we make water safe to drink? The multiplication and division have a real purpose.
  • Bring in the bigger picture. If a third grader can figure out what a community needs, so can the people making decisions. The children’s math becomes something they can use to speak up.
  • Celebrate water protectors. Students learn about Josephine Mandamin and Autumn Peltier, Anishinaabe water protectors from Wiikwemkoong, Ontario, which the curriculum itself encourages.

To see how the idea works across a whole classroom, I built it out as a full kit: whole group slides and a packet, the small-group phase packets I use for intervention (Pre-Phase through Phase 3), an enrichment project, and a Logic Lab sheet where a repeat loop becomes multiplication. It is aligned to Grade 3 expectations B2.1, B2.2, B2.6 and B2.7.

I’m still learning. I want to understand more about how First Nations schools use the Ontario curriculum, and I believe lessons like this are best designed together with communities. That’s what I’d like to learn next.

My goal is to create curriculum where every child, especially children in underserved communities, can see themselves in the math and feel capable and proud.

A note on how I work: the ideas and the design are mine, and I use AI tools to help produce the materials, such as drafting practice problems and laying out pages.

Thank you for reading.

Genevieve Campuzano Garrido
Genna · Grade 3 educator
